MBA Apologia: Open Letter to Generalists in the AI era

*context: I spoke with friend about advice about being a BizOps generalist in the AI era. I was concerned where MBAs would fit in these new org paradigms. This is an attempt at codifying the response and trying to clarify it.*

It's hard to name an era while you're still in it, but I think we can safely christen this the AI era. In the age of AI, precarity of employment is a key part of the discourse. Its hard to disentangle progress narratives and determine fact from fiction. I don't try to predict the future, but I do look for early signs, in attempts to skate where the puck is going. 

See, I don't really have a marketable career per se. I'm in [flounder mode](https://joincolossus.com/article/flounder-mode/). I'm what you would call a generalist. I live in a space inhabited by MBAs (derogatory) and ex-consultants/bankers. This is/was a valuable skillset in the software era, and there was a clear need for this type of person early on who could hold-up different functions and then specialize or become a pseudo-COO. This space is no longer as well-defined. 

Teams are leaner. It seems like either you're building the product or selling the product. In the AI era, having a 6-month lead in developing a product is no longer a moat. Start-ups need to find other sources of competitive advantage, like GTM (better distribution, simpler pricing, cracked sales/marketing).

In this [insider account](https://calv.info/openai-reflections) of culture at OpenAI by Calvin French-Owen, he writes about the org structure of the team behind the Codex launch: "We were a senior team of ~8 engineers, ~4 researchers, 2 designers, 2 GTM and a PM." This seems to be the [two pizza](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/TwoPizzaTeam.html) team of the AI era. Earlier stage teams meld some of these roles and there's probably slight differences based on type of company offering (more on this later).

Where generalists fit into AI companies is where they've fit in previous era of start-ups. One of the most effective uses of  AI will be business process automation. Just like how forward deployed engineers are en vogue to do and observe customer process to build bespoke tooling for them, generalists can do that for internal tooling and functions. For instance, this person can dog food internal GTM flows and add structure and protocols to help make these processes more systematic since they understand the whole as well as its parts. There's a lot of scaffolding needed to make AI tools work right now. Someone has to build that scaffolding and troubleshoot it until dev resources are justified to build something more robust. Many internal stakeholders have myopic view of their process in silos (for instance finance with month-end close or quarterly planning cycles). As you grow a company you accrue a sort of "operations" debt, and you have to build something rather than nothing (policies, processes etc.,). You need generalists who can take the macro view and be the glue between departments. 

Before we discuss how this role looks, it's helpful to share a taxonomy of current offerings how I see them:

Horizontal:
	- Compute and infrastructure (cloud/accelerators), which underpins the stack but is distinct from models and apps.
	- Foundation models and APIs (general‑purpose capabilities).
	- Application stack/tooling (orchestration, retrieval, eval/observability, vector stores).
	- Horizontal agents/copilots (cross‑functional assistants).

Vertical:
	- Vertical software (industry apps with deep integrations).
	- Vertical services (Service‑as‑Software): outcome‑priced, automation‑led services often with humans‑in‑the‑loop, distinct from seat‑licensed SaaS.

For the horizontal offerings, generalists mainly fit into either GTM or Product (Ops). For instance, having expertise selling to an ICP, or building for a similar customer would be a great differentiator. For vertical offerings, there's more versatility. Generalist would be good as a cross between sales and a solution architect for early customers (Forward-deployed Ops?). Generalist that have worked within the vertical can occupy much more diverse roles. For instances, ex-lawyers working for AI lawyer offering can be human-in-loop and help automate more of the workflow, help industrialize and make a more refined product. They can provide valuable input as product and GTM roles with customer insights and incumbent offerings. If I were to "skate where the puck is going", these are opportunities I would think about.
