## The Craft of Great Research

Research is done in many different contexts for many different ends. I want to share some principles which I've learned, which can help improve your life through research. As a disclaimer this is not a rigorous framework for conducting research in an academic context. Instead, these are principles of research that can broadly be applied to gathering and organizing information in many different contexts, academic or not. Whatever your ends are -- researching for work, pleasure, or creative pursuit -- this will help inform your approach and getting the most out of it.
## Why

If you're able to conduct great research, by gathering and organizing information it can impact every aspect of your life. Many areas of your life are enriched by learning or gaining an advantage through exploiting information asymmetries. Some examples of this are:

- Many competitive games like chess, sports, or video games often have a META (Most  Effective Tactic Available). Knowing what this tactics or set of tactics are early will allow you to exploit them until those strategies. That is until they become the predominant tactic, at which point know the best counter plays against those tactics becomes the asymmetric advantage. 
- In business having access to the strategies at the frontier (efficiency, technical, strategic) could lead to outsized gains. For instance, you could monetize this expertise via consulting/investing or gain outsized increase in valuation by employing these tactics. If you don't know them, you will likely have to pay for them to remain competitive (for instance, META having to pay for Researchers from top AI labs to gain institutional knowledge from the frontier).
- High stakes negotiations, in power and politics are usual hinge on information asymmetry -- i.e., knowing what the counterparty interests and what's at stake for them, and exploiting that.
- More simply, the value of information at pivotal points in your life can completely change the trajectory of life. For instance, gaining a mentor in college or early in work, navigating a healthcare journey with multiple perspectives or opinions, or being influenced to travel to a place after reading about someone's experience.

Although some of the most valuable and reliable information is gated behind resources like money or social capital, "you can just do things" is surprisingly effective tactic. A lot of knowledge is democratized with the internet and surprisingly asking people for coffee or cold emails can be game changing. Just as knowledge is democratized, it is also abundant both in terms of good and bad.  The key differentiator is being able to discern quality of the information and then being able to act on it.

> *“From this day on, no credit will be given for predicting rain; only credit for building an ark.”*
> - Ben Horowitz, quoting an old manager (source: Lenny's Podcast)

## Research Defined

Research for the purpose of this essay, means gathering, and organizing information from a variety of sources and acting upon it. For instance, it can look like sourcing recommendations on what to read next from friends, book reviews and syllabi or it could be writing a prompt with your blood work into an LLM to prepare for an annual check up with your family physician. In these instances, your experience is enhanced greatly, over someone who does not prepare in this way. If you're skillful in gathering and acting on information quickly, and consistently you'll be able to apply this skill to become successful in many domains in record time. 

Like many skills this improves over time, and you get faster as you learn to recognize authoritative sources and fine tune what you're looking for, what are the best questions to ask etc., What follows are general principles that I think can flatten this learning curve.

## Principles of Great Research
### 1. Great research is just-in-time

Great research is actionable. The world is constantly in a state of flux. Acting fast is an advantage. Certain types of knowledge grow obsolete quickly. Doing great research, you should be working back from a problem and decide on the minimum viable knowledge you need to make a decision or take action. You should be constantly seeking to stress test this, by asking "to what end?". Learning is the ultimate currency here. The shorter the feedback loop, the faster the rate of improvement will be.

Just-in-time research is valuable because you don't need to expend cognitive load to hold it. You can action it immediately, and refine the good and bad parts. Eventually, this information or knowledge becomes second nature or part of your subconscious.  Be careful of falling into the trap of "knowledge" materialism, or hoarding information for information sake. From my experience in Universities in liberal arts programs, is this peacock show of vibrant feathers to show fitness and prowess -- similarly being able to cite narrow and esoteric theorists and knowledge as a display of status in terms of information diet but that you have so much free time to go into the long-tail of knowledge that is only useful in like 3 contexts.

However, I want to say truly creative and ground breaking research and knowledge gain can feel like this at time as you're trying to string together new discoveries and make novel connections between disparate fields. Ultimately, the amount of "novelty" knowledge or knowledge you're hoping connects into something bigger should be contained lest you fall into knowledge materialism trap. Just-in-case knowledge is a luxury, treat it as such.

---
### 2. Great research done

Perfect is the enemy of possible. Good enough is the enemy of great.  Great research is shipped, out into the world, consumed by others and built upon (by you or others). As long as you put forth your best work with epistemic honesty, it will almost always benefit by being out into the world. Sometimes, the idealized version of what the research could be, gets in the way of what it is. And at times being afraid to confront what it is is an ego-defense mechanism. As in being able to confront that has its faults, it might not find an audience or be obsolete for this current time. But tell me what is better -- spending 4 years in a PhD program toiling away at pet project that you believe is important only to launch it after its "complete" or write it in public throughout and figure out its not as interesting or important for the time being and freeing up the time to pursue something more promising (albeit less sexy?) but resonates or get's reactions out of others and moves the discourse further in some ways? Obviously you shouldn't use external validation to guide your research but also its important to avoid personal vanity projects. This happens in organizations frequently where they get caught up in "current thing" syndrome -- like with AI, six sigma or zero-based-budgeting.

It is important to fight this feeling of perfection or guarding work until it is "complete". I find that the best research is that which is done, just putting pen to paper, gathering information, talking to people and then taking action or shipping your work out into the world. Once it is out, you gain valuable insights and feedback to hone it. You quickly realize what its not by turning over stones and can move on to turning other ones (opportunity cost). By gaining information via feedback, you hone your overall process as well being able to be much more prolific in your output. You build the muscle memory of being able to put out research that exceeds a certain quality bar at a reasonable speed. At the end of the day moving beyond information gathering and process is the ultimate goal and losing site of that is detrimental to the whole project. Sometimes, your taste grows faster than skill or craft and this leads you to delaying the creative act or not being able to make the jump to start and finish a project. 

Somethings are open and shut and other things will be opportunities to make it evergreen. For instance, you might write about your worldview on X and it will resonate with some and not with others, pieces of knowledge will surface that you may not have considered. Therefore your research is better for this. You finished v1 and now can write v1.1 -- whereas trying to craft something perfect first shot is not always great (there's cases where this makes sense where you don't want your art tainted but theses are rare cases and you need to be careful about vanity projects like these).

There are fringe cases where the opposite is true. Where if you're cultivating something which you want to preserve the creative integrity of what you're building in an uncompromised way. You chip away at it over a long time horizon and don't want to corrupt it with outside influence. I think these creative pursuits are important. However, there are few instances where this method would be preferable over iterating based on external feedback.

> Lightning McQueen: All right, Luigi, give me the best set of black walls you've got.
> Luigi: No, no, no! You don't-a know what you want! Luigi know what you want
> -- *Cars, 2006*

For every solitary work of uncompromising genius, there are probably thousands of counter examples. But in rare cases, the world needs slow, sacred and stubborn, cathedral-building projects. It is equal important to have conviction in these cases.

---
###  3. Great research is as evergreen as it needs to be

Great research is a life-long project of insatiable curiosity. But not every research project needs to be. Each research project should get its own due. 

For instance, there may be projects which are fairly simple, one-time affairs that are open for a time and then closed. For instance, you're deciding which type of coffee maker to buy, you know you enjoy espresso, so you do your research and trial a variety of grinders and machines, and settle on a setup that works for you. This might be the full extent of this project for you. For others, it might require constant monitoring and upgrading, making constant tweaks to their set up. For some people this is part of the joy of brewing coffee or it is there career. For others, the most convenient option is best.

The point is in research evergreen is seen as the gold standard, one must always stay abreast of changing information and update their research to incorporate it to make sure it retains accuracy. While this is true, the principle is meant to be applied judiciously for:

>Life is short,
>and craft long,
>opportunity fleeting,
>experimentations perilous,
>and judgment difficult.
>--  *Hippocrates*

and

> Rob: Derek, why do you care about these people? They don't care about you. None of them. They don't even know you because you haven't shown them. Every day, you'll wake up, and there'll be less of you. You live your life for them, and they don't even see you. You don't even see yourself.
> Rob: We don't get a lot of things to really care about.
> -- *Pig (2021)*

The point being is that for research that matters to you and that you will act on for a long time needs to remain evergreen. For instance, if your career depends on it -- you should take measures to speak with peers, listen and watch others, read what they right and update your worldview accordingly. There is research where the cost of being wrong is higher than usual or there are finite or irreversible actions and consequence, in these instances, you need to be more diligent in upkeeping your model of reality. Part of great research is making ideas robust, looking at counterfactuals, seeing ways in which the opposite of what you think could be true. In this way, great research can be a life-long pursuit, and you will need curiosity to see it through. One example of these types of projects is Robert Caro's biography of Lyndon B. Johnson, which he has spent 36 years researching the life of one person. 

In closing, doing great research is not a result of some innate genius, but rather about having ideas, testing them through practice systematically, and iterating based on results. Important part of research is about learning in public and having the courage to pursue important questions with [beginners mind.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoshin) I could never do this topic justice, so I'll leave you with a curated list of the very best reading on the craft of research.
